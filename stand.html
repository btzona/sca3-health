Where things stand

Care, trials, and family planning, as reviewed on 5 October 2026.

Not medical advice. Trial statuses change. Recheck ClinicalTrials.gov and the sponsor’s page before any decision about testing, pregnancy, or a study. If you are new to the disease itself, start with About SCA3.

The short version

SCA3 is inherited. If a parent carries a disease-causing expansion in ATXN3, each child has about a 50% chance of inheriting it. There is still no approved drug that prevents the disease, cures it, or has been shown to slow the underlying nerve-cell damage. Supportive care, especially balance work and attention to falls and swallowing, helps people who have symptoms. For a future pregnancy, embryo testing and prenatal testing are real options. They do not change whether a child who is already born inherited the expansion.

The laboratory pipeline is more active than it was a decade ago. An antisense drug, VO659, is in an early trial for adults who already have mild to moderate symptoms. A second intrathecal drug, Servier’s S233107, was authorised as a placebo-controlled study. The public CTIS page, read 17 October 2026, lists that trial as end of trial on 11 September 2026, before recruitment started. Neither is a treatment for children, and neither is approved.

Care that exists now

Standard care is multidisciplinary and aimed at symptoms and safety. Physical therapy works on balance, gait, strength, and falls. Occupational therapy helps with daily tasks and the house. Speech-language pathology helps with speech and, importantly, with swallowing. Aspiration, food or liquid entering the airway, is a serious late risk. Nutrition, bone health, mood, sleep, vision, and driving safety belong in the same plan. Exercise is recommended for function. It is not established as a way to change the course of the disease in a gene carrier who has no symptoms.

Some symptoms can be treated with ordinary medicines: parkinsonism in the people who respond to levodopa, spasticity, dystonia, cramps, nerve pain, sleep problems, depression. No single oral “ataxia drug” is accepted as disease-modifying. Historical candidates such as riluzole have had limited or mixed evidence.

In November 2025 the U.S. FDA issued a Complete Response Letter for Biohaven’s troriluzole (VYGLXIA) as a treatment for spinocerebellar ataxia. That is a rejection of the application, not an approval. As of this review it is not an approved SCA treatment in the United States. Supplements, antioxidant cocktails, and stem-cell clinics abroad are not supported as prevention or as disease modification.

VO659

NCT05822908 is an open-label, multiple-ascending-dose study of safety and pharmacokinetics. For SCA3 it asks for a repeat of at least 61 CAG and for ataxia that is measurable but not advanced (SARA score roughly 3 to 18). It does not enroll people who carry the gene and have no ataxia, and it does not enroll children. Primary completion was estimated around 2028. In February 2026 the company said a twice-yearly dosing group had started, and that the FDA had cleared a U.S. study. Treat those statements as company updates.

S233107

Servier’s trial page describes a first-in-human study: several dose levels against placebo, then an open-label extension, injections into the spinal fluid. The public eligibility notes call for adults 18 to 65 with symptomatic SCA3, at least 60 CAG on one allele, a SARA score of at least 3, and the ability to walk without a continuous aid. Symptom onset before age 18 is excluded. No efficacy results exist yet. Read on 17 October 2026, the public CTIS page lists the overall status as end of trial on 11 September 2026. Denmark, France, Spain, and Portugal are marked Ended, reason Sponsor Decision, and the recruitment columns are blank. The same page still shows an estimated recruitment start of 1 September 2026 and an estimated end of 31 October 2030. Servier’s trial page still said not yet recruiting that day and still said centres in the US and Japan would be added. The study is not on Open trials.

What is easy to misread

  • A biomarker change in Huntington disease is not a demonstration that the same drug slows SCA3.
  • Stem-cell products and brain-stimulation studies are not gene silencing. Two device studies, temporal interference stimulation (NCT07743866) and cerebellar deep-brain stimulation (NCT07752095), were not yet recruiting at this review.
  • Natural-history studies do not give a drug. They matter because later trials need people, measures, and biomarkers. CRC-SCA is the main U.S. and Canadian network. READISCA (NCT03487367) was a readiness study; its registry status was listed as unknown at the last check.

Reproductive choices

These options apply when an adult is planning a pregnancy. They do not treat a child who is already born.

  • IVF with PGT-M. Embryos can be tested for the familial ATXN3 expansion, and an embryo without it can be transferred. The method is established for SCA3 in specialized laboratories. A 2024 paper describes a direct test plus linked markers, and a birth after transfer. Residual technical risk is low with a good lab and is not zero, so confirmatory prenatal testing is often offered. The workup usually needs DNA from the carrier and sometimes from relatives. Cost, age, and ovarian reserve all matter.
  • Prenatal diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, after a natural conception. That brings procedure risk and a decision about the pregnancy.
  • Donor egg or sperm from a donor who does not carry the familial expansion, or adoption.
  • Conceiving without testing, which leaves the 50% chance in place if one parent is a carrier.
  • Some couples use linkage methods that avoid telling an at-risk partner their own result. That is a counseling conversation, not a workaround to arrange alone.

Many adults want to know their own result before they choose among these paths. Predictive testing in adults follows a structured counseling process, on the model used for Huntington disease. There is no lifestyle, diet, or supplement that prevents SCA3 or stops a gene from being inherited.

Children and teenagers at risk

SCA3 usually starts in adulthood. For a child or teenager who has no neurologic symptoms, the prevailing guidance is to wait on predictive genetic testing until that person can decide as an adult. The reasons are practical and ethical:

  • No approved treatment can be started in childhood on the basis of a positive test.
  • A result, positive or negative, takes away the child’s later choice about whether to know.
  • A result can change how a child is seen at home, at school, and around insurance. In the United States, health-insurance protections do not cover every kind of insurance.
  • If a child has symptoms and is tested “just in case,” a positive SCA3 result can distract from other, treatable causes.

The National Society of Genetic Counselors discourages predictive testing of minors for adult-onset conditions, and says the balance could change if treatment changes. Huntington-disease protocols, which SCA3 clinics generally follow, commonly use 18 as the minimum age for predictive testing. GeneReviews uses the same frame.

Parents can still do useful things without testing a child. Keep the affected parent’s lab report, with both allele sizes. Have a relationship with an ataxia clinic and a genetic counselor. Talk about the family history in ordinary language, so it is not a secret. Pay attention to real neurologic changes — persistent trouble walking, falls, speech or swallowing changes, clear eye-movement problems — and get those evaluated clinically. Testing a symptomatic child, after a proper workup, is a different question from testing a healthy child because a parent is afraid. When a young adult asks, the sound path is counseling first, time to think, and support after the result, whatever it is. It is their decision.

The National Ataxia Foundation has described counseling and testing programs for some at-risk relatives of a person with confirmed SCA3. Age rules are the program’s, not this page’s. Ask NAF what they currently require. That offer does not overturn the guidance against routine predictive testing of asymptomatic minors.

If a safe preventive treatment someday had to start in adolescence, this advice would need to be rewritten. As of October 2026, the gene-targeted drugs in or near the clinic are early studies in symptomatic adults.

What would change this page

  • An approved treatment whose label includes people who carry the expansion but do not yet have ataxia, or includes adolescents.
  • A well-designed trial that enrolls asymptomatic expansion carriers, especially under 18.
  • A change in professional guidance on testing minors, because a result would change medical care.
