For parents

Talking with your children about SCA3

What to say, what can wait, and where to turn when the talking is hard. This page is for you. It is not written for a child to read alone.

If SCA3 is in your family, you have probably wondered what your children should know, and when. You do not have to give a lecture. You do not have to have every answer. What helps is keeping the illness from becoming a secret they have to piece together alone.

Adults 18 and over who grew up with this can read If your mom or dad has SCA3. Inheritance, testing, and pregnancy are on For families.

Why telling them early helps

The clearest research on this question comes from families living with Huntington’s disease, a different illness that is passed on in the same way: each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance. Researchers in Scotland talked with 33 young people, ages 9 to 28. Those who had grown up knowing about the illness from early on seemed to cope better. How they were told mattered. Some had always known. Some were told a little at a time. Some were kept in the dark, or heard it all at once with a new diagnosis. Timing and the way it was said mattered more than a perfect script.

That is Huntington’s research, not an SCA3 study. It is still the best published look at what openness does for children in a family with this kind of risk.

Match what you say to their age

You can tell the truth in a small dose. A young child can know that a parent or grandparent has an illness that affects walking and speech, and that doctors are involved. You can add more when they ask. It is all right to say “I don’t know yet” and come back to the question.

Two booklets written for parents in Huntington’s families walk through words for different ages. They are not SCA3 guides. They are useful because the family pattern is the same kind of 50/50.

  • Talking with young people about Huntington’s disease, from the Scottish Huntington’s Association.
  • Talking With Kids About HD, from the Huntington’s Disease Society of America.

When they ask, “Will I get it?”

The honest answer is a chance, not a yes or a no. If a parent has SCA3, each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the expanded gene and a 50% chance of not inheriting it. Sons and daughters have the same chance. The chance starts over with each child. One brother or sister testing negative does not mean another child has it. “One of you must have it” is not how this inheritance works.

Looking like the parent does not change the chance. The same eyes, the same laugh, or a similar walk are not the SCA3 gene.

A gene test cannot tell anyone the age symptoms would start. People with similar results can start at very different ages. In a large group of affected people from the Azores, the average onset was 37. A large Brazilian group found an average of about 36. An average is not a deadline for your child.

Testing is their decision, and it waits for adulthood

Predictive testing means a gene test when there are no neurologic symptoms, to learn whether someone inherited the expansion. The National Society of Genetic Counselors, in a statement revised in 2025, generally discourages that test in children and teenagers who have no symptoms, when the condition starts in adulthood. There is no approved SCA3 treatment that would be started in childhood because of a positive test.

The choice belongs to the person who would be tested. Guidance that SCA3 clinics generally follow, written for Huntington’s disease, says the decision is theirs alone. No parent, partner, or doctor should push them. That guidance uses 18 as the usual minimum age. A child who has questions can still talk with a genetic counselor about what testing involves. Talking is not the same as testing.

Never testing is a real choice, and it is the common one. In one program in the Azores, about 1 in 5 at-risk people completed predictive testing for Machado-Joseph disease. In Huntington’s disease, a different condition, a whole-population study found that about 15% had tested after 20 years. Those are different measures. Both say the same thing about the choice: most people at risk do not test.

Someone can decide “not now” and come back years later. They can also stop after counseling has started, even after a blood sample, and choose not to hear the result. Results are given in person, not by phone or mail. In the Azores, people who went through SCA3 testing were not more depressed or anxious a year later than they had been before, whether the result was positive or negative.

Free counseling through NAF

When an adult in your family is ready to talk it through, the National Ataxia Foundation offers free virtual genetic counseling and genetic testing for people at risk who have a direct relative with a positive SCA3 genetic test. The contact on its SCA3 page is research@ataxia.org. That page does not state an age rule. The program is the place to ask. A counselor near you is listed in the National Society of Genetic Counselors directory.

Caregiving and grief at home

Illness at home pulls children into helping. A study of teenagers in Huntington’s families in the United States and Canada found that 24 of 32 described doing some caregiving, often while knowing they might develop the disease themselves. They felt responsible for decisions they had no authority to make. Later work with those families found that getting information, doing something else for a while, and acting for the ill parent helped more than bottling feelings up. Support from outside the family was often missing.

If your child is helping you, or helping their other parent, they still need time that is not caregiving, and someone to talk with who is not the person they are looking after. In the UK, Ataxia UK’s branches welcome family members and carers, and it has an online carers group.

Grief does not wait for a death. It can start while a parent is changing. The Dougy Center publishes free grief resources for teenagers, including articles, activities, and a bill of rights for teens who have a family member living with a serious illness. Its programs are offered at no cost. The in-person groups are in Portland, Oregon. The pages online are free.

If someone in your home is in crisis in the United States, call or text 988. The line is free, and it is answered all day and night.
