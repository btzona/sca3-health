Family questions

For families at risk

Inheritance, testing, pregnancy, and how to talk about the disease. These are options and prevailing guidance, not instructions.

Talking with your children is for parents. If your mom or dad has SCA3 is for adults 18 and over. What the disease is, in plain language, is on About SCA3.

Predictive testing of a child or teenager who has no neurologic symptoms is generally deferred until that person can decide as an adult. The reasons are in Children and teenagers. Nothing on this page is a reason to test a child.

Free counseling and testing through NAF

The National Ataxia Foundation’s SCA3 page, read 17 October 2026, has a section titled “Free Genetic Testing for SCA1, 2, 3.” The text says NAF is offering free virtual genetic counseling and genetic testing for individuals who are at risk for SCA3. It says individuals who have a direct relative who has genetically tested positive for SCA3 are eligible. The page gives research@ataxia.org as the contact. Age limits, cost after the offer, and which countries are included are the program’s to state.

Studies that include people before symptoms

These are readiness and natural-history studies. Eligibility below is what each site stated on 17 October 2026.

ESMI

The European Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 3 Initiative says its cohort includes people with a diagnosis of SCA3, individuals at risk of developing SCA3 such as first-degree relatives of patients, and healthy controls. It describes annual ataxia rating scales, blood samples, cerebrospinal fluid samples, and MRI, used to follow the disease and to prepare later treatment trials. The site asks people who have SCA3, or a close relative who does, to write to office-human-imaging@dzne.de. It does not publish an age range on that page.

READISCA

READISCA describes itself as an NIH-funded clinical trial readiness study for SCA1 and SCA3. The home page says the study has been collecting, and will keep collecting, information in pre-ataxic and early ataxic carriers of SCA1 and SCA3 mutations. The aims page says one aim was to establish a group of early-stage and symptomless SCA1 and SCA3 individuals. A protocol link on that site was not available on 17 October 2026. The contact printed on the home page is hmaiapps@houstonmethodist.org.

How it is inherited

SCA3 is autosomal dominant. One expanded copy of ATXN3 is enough. If a parent carries a disease-causing expansion, each child has about a 50% chance of inheriting it and a 50% chance of not inheriting it. The chance is the same for sons and daughters, and it is reset at each pregnancy.

The repeat is unstable. It more often grows than shrinks from parent to child, a pattern called anticipation, which can mean earlier onset in the next generation. That is not a forecast for any one child. Even when the repeat length is known, the age symptoms will start cannot be predicted precisely. There is no diet, supplement, or lifestyle that stops the expansion from being inherited.

Predictive testing in adults

Predictive testing means a gene test in an adult who has no neurologic symptoms, to learn whether they inherited the expansion. Many adults want that result before they make decisions about work, insurance, or a pregnancy. Others decide not to know. Both are recognized paths.

The usual process, which SCA3 clinics generally model on Huntington disease, is structured counseling: time to think before the test, and support after the result, whatever it is. GeneReviews uses that frame. A result is the person’s own information.

Children and teenagers

SCA3 usually starts in adulthood. For a child or teenager who has no neurologic symptoms, the prevailing guidance is to wait on predictive genetic testing until that person can decide as an adult. The National Society of Genetic Counselors discourages predictive testing of minors for adult-onset conditions, and says the balance could change if a treatment would start in childhood. Huntington-disease protocols, which SCA3 clinics generally follow, commonly use 18 as the minimum age. GeneReviews uses the same frame.

The reasons given in that guidance are practical:

  • No approved treatment can be started in childhood on the basis of a positive test. As of October 2026, the gene-targeted drugs in or near the clinic are early studies in symptomatic adults.
  • A result, positive or negative, takes away the child’s later choice about whether to know.
  • A result can change how a child is seen at home, at school, and around insurance. In the United States, health-insurance protections do not cover every kind of insurance.
  • If a child has symptoms and is tested “just in case,” a positive SCA3 result can distract from other, treatable causes.

Testing a symptomatic child, after a proper workup, is a different question from testing a healthy child because a parent is afraid. When a young adult asks, the sound path in the guidance is counseling first, time to think, and support after the result. It is their decision.

Reproductive options

These options apply when an adult is planning a pregnancy. They do not treat a child who is already born. This list is not a ranking. Cost, age, health, beliefs, and the laws where a person lives all change what is possible. A genetics team is the place to sort them.

  • IVF with PGT-M. Embryos can be tested for the familial ATXN3 expansion, and an embryo without it can be transferred. The method is established for SCA3 in specialized laboratories. A 2024 paper describes a direct test plus linked markers, and a birth after transfer. Residual technical risk is low with a good lab and is not zero, so confirmatory prenatal testing is often offered. The workup usually needs DNA from the carrier and sometimes from relatives.
  • Prenatal diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, after a natural conception. That brings procedure risk and a decision about the pregnancy.
  • Donor egg or sperm from a donor who does not carry the familial expansion, or adoption.
  • Conceiving without testing, which leaves the 50% chance in place if one parent is a carrier.
  • Some couples use linkage methods that avoid telling an at-risk partner their own result. That is a counseling conversation, not a workaround to arrange alone.

Finding a genetic counselor

In the United States and Canada, the National Society of Genetic Counselors hosts a directory: Find a Genetic Counselor. An ataxia clinic can also make the introduction. The National Ataxia Foundation keeps a map of neurologists and specialty clinics and a list of Ataxia Centers of Excellence. NAF says it does not verify every physician on the broader neurologist list. Ask what a clinic currently offers before you travel.

Useful things to bring: the affected relative’s lab report, with both allele sizes, and a short family tree. A counselor can work with an incomplete record. They cannot invent a missing result.

Talking with children

Parents can do useful things without testing a child. Keep the lab report. Have a relationship with an ataxia clinic and a counselor. Talk about the family history in ordinary language, so it is not a secret and so it is not the only thing the child hears about themselves.

The amount of detail can match the question. A young child may only need to know that a parent or grandparent has an illness that affects walking and speech, and that doctors are involved. An older child who asks whether they will get it can hear the 50% figure, and that a test is a decision for adulthood, with help. It is all right to say “I don’t know yet” and to come back to the question.

Pay attention to real neurologic changes — persistent trouble walking, falls, speech or swallowing changes, clear eye-movement problems — and get those evaluated clinically. Fear is not the same thing as a new symptom.

If a safe preventive treatment someday had to start in adolescence, this advice would need to be rewritten. As of October 2026, that is not the situation described in the sources below. A longer page for parents is Talking with your children about SCA3.
