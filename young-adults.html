For adults 18 and over

If your mom or dad has SCA3

This page is for you if you are 18 or older and a parent has SCA3, or had it. Nothing here asks you to decide today.

Maybe you’ve known about it as long as you can remember. Maybe you found out last week. Maybe you’ve watched your parent change, or helped take care of them, or lost them. However you got here, you’re not the only one. There’s no right way to feel about it, and nothing you need to do today.

What follows is what research and counselors have learned from people in families with this kind of risk. Read what helps and skip what doesn’t. You can come back any time. The note from the person who built this site is on the home page. It is signed by a father.

What 50/50 really means

When a parent has SCA3, each of their children has a 50% chance of inheriting it and a 50% chance of not inheriting it. A lot of people say 50/50 doesn’t feel like 50/50. It feels bigger. That feeling is common. It doesn’t mean you’re bad at math.

  • It’s your own coin flip. If you have a brother or sister, each of you has a separate 50/50. One of you testing negative does not make it more likely that the other has it. “One of us must have it” is not how this works.
  • Looking like your parent doesn’t change your odds. The same eyes, the same laugh, a similar walk: those are not the SCA3 gene.
  • A family “pattern” doesn’t predict you. If it has seemed to be the oldest, or the girls, or one side of the family, that is how it happened to fall. Sons and daughters have the same chance.
  • A gene test can’t say when symptoms would start. People with similar results can start at very different ages. In a large Azorean group the average onset was 37, and in a large Brazilian group it was about 36. An average is not a deadline.

You don’t have to decide anything

You might wonder whether to have a gene test to find out if you inherited SCA3. Some people want to know. Some people never do. Some people decide “not now” and come back years later. All of those are real choices. Not testing is the most common one.

In one program in the Azores, about 1 in 5 at-risk people completed predictive testing for Machado-Joseph disease. In Huntington’s disease, a different condition with the same kind of 50/50, a whole-population study found that about 15% had tested after 20 years. Those numbers measure different things. They agree that most people at risk do not test.

If you do want to explore testing, this is how it usually works:

  • It’s your decision, and only yours. Not your parents’, not a partner’s, not a doctor’s. The guidance SCA3 clinics generally follow says the choice belongs to the person being tested, with no pressure from anyone else.
  • It’s a process, not a blood draw. You meet a genetic counselor more than once. At least a month between the information and the decision is the usual recommendation. You get the result in person, not by phone or mail. Support comes after, whatever the answer is.
  • You can pause, stop, or change your mind. Even after giving a sample, you can decide not to hear the result.
  • Timing matters. A hard season or a big celebration is a rough time to start. Bring someone you trust to the appointments.

In the Azores, people who went through SCA3 testing were not more depressed or anxious a year later than they had been before, whether the result was positive or negative. Five years later, the average wellbeing score among carriers in that group sat just inside the range the researchers called well-being. The average was lower once symptoms had started, and about half of the people scored in a stressed range. Support after the result still mattered.

When you want to talk it through, the National Ataxia Foundation offers free virtual genetic counseling and testing for people at risk who have a parent or other direct relative with a positive SCA3 test. The contact on its SCA3 page is research@ataxia.org. You can also find a genetic counselor near you.

The “was that a symptom?” thing

A lot of people at risk scan themselves. A stumble is usually just a stumble. If something keeps happening, a neurologist’s exam answers it better than watching yourself does. The National Ataxia Foundation’s SCA3 page says a neurologist is often the most useful specialist for symptoms that might be SCA3.

If you’re helping take care of your parent

Research with teenagers in Huntington’s families found that most of them were already doing some caregiving, and that they felt responsible for decisions they had no authority to make. Keeping it all in was common and didn’t help much. Getting information, and having something in life that wasn’t the illness, helped more. Outside support was often missing.

If that was your childhood, or if care is your life now, you are allowed to have hours that are not about the illness. In the UK, Ataxia UK’s branches welcome family members and carers, and it runs an online carers group. Both are for relatives, not only for people who have ataxia.

If your parent has died

Grief comes in waves. It can also start before a death, while someone is changing. The Dougy Center offers grief support for young adults and families. Its programs are at no cost. The in-person groups are in Portland, Oregon, and it publishes free resources online.

The person who built this site wrote about losing a parent to SCA3. That note is on the home page.

Friends, dating, and who you tell

Your story is yours. You can tell a friend or a partner, or not tell them. You can tell them later. Before you do, it helps to know what you want them to understand, what you hope they will do with it, and whether you’re ready for whatever they say back.

On São Miguel, in the Azores, interviews published in 2025 found that stigma around Machado-Joseph disease had been intense, and that openness has been growing. The patient association there was a big part of that change. Talking about it in a family is not the same as owing the story to everyone else.

Work, plans, and “is there time?”

It is easy to feel you have to do everything now. It is also easy to feel there is no point in planning. Symptoms can start in childhood or late in life. The 30s are a frequent range in the large studies, not a clock running on you.

Someday, if you want children

You don’t have to decide this now. If you want children later, there are real options: IVF with testing of embryos for the familial expansion, testing a pregnancy, a donor egg or sperm from someone who does not carry that expansion, or adoption. There is also the choice to conceive without testing. A genetic counselor can walk through them when you want that conversation, including years from now. The practical list is on For families.

Your rights in the United States

A federal law, GINA, limits how genetic information can be used. Genetic information includes family medical history, not only a test you take yourself.

  • Health insurers can’t use it to decide whether to cover you, or what to charge you.
  • Employers with 15 or more employees can’t use it in hiring, firing, pay, or job assignments, and can’t require a genetic test.
  • It does not cover life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance.
  • It does not cover employers with fewer than 15 employees.
  • It does not stop the U.S. military from using genetic information in employment decisions. TRICARE eligibility depends on that employment.

Because life, disability, and long-term care insurance sit outside GINA, some people sort those out before they test. A genetic counselor can help you think that through. The law is explained by the National Human Genome Research Institute.

Where the research is

There is no approved treatment that stops or slows SCA3. Doctors can treat many of the symptoms. Gene-targeted medicines are in early studies in adults who already have symptoms. They are not approved treatments. Studies such as ESMI and READISCA include people before symptoms, so that later trials have something to measure. The plain account of care and the pipeline is on Where things stand.

Find your people

There isn’t yet an English-language group just for adults who are at risk of SCA3 and don’t have symptoms. These are the closest real places. Each line says who the group is actually for.

  • NAF Community Support Group (US). A private Facebook group for people affected by ataxia, including family and friends. The event listings say the cost is free. NAF support groups.
  • NAF groups for ages 18–24 and 25–35. These are for people who have ataxia. The pages say you must have ataxia yourself, and that parents, spouses, and friends can’t join. They are free and meet online. The 25–35 group, called The Dominoes, meets twice a month on Zoom.
  • Ataxia UK branches and carers group. Family members, partners, and carers are welcome at branches. There is a separate online carers group. Those are for relatives. Ataxia UK’s younger group is only for people who already have ataxia. Branches. Carers group.
  • DHAG Junge Community (Germany, in German). For people up to about 35. The page names young people with ataxia, people at risk, and relatives. Cost is not stated. Contact jugend@ataxie.de. Junge Erkrankte.
  • AAADMJ (Azores, mostly in Portuguese). Support for people with Machado-Joseph disease, their families, and carers. There is an English toggle. Support line +351 296 281 628. The association.
  • ABAHE (Brazil, in Portuguese). A patient group with a support page. Signing up for its registry is free. Support.
  • MJD Foundation (Australia). Partners with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in specific regions to support families living with Machado-Joseph disease and SCA7. It is not a general service for every family. mjd.org.au.

Questions you might have

Does looking like my parent change my odds?

No. Each child has a separate 50% chance. Resemblance is not the gene.

If my sibling tests negative, does that mean I have it?

No. Their result doesn’t move your chance. It stays 50/50 until you test, if you ever do.

Is it okay not to want to know?

Yes. Most people at risk never test. “Not now” is also a decision. You can leave it there.

If it’s too much right now and you are in the United States, call or text 988. The line is free, and it is answered all day and night. Outside the United States, use the local crisis line where you live.
