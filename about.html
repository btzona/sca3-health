Ataxin-3

About SCA3, for someone starting from the beginning.

Ledger About SCA3 Where things stand

This page is education, not medical advice. It does not diagnose anyone or recommend a test, a drug, or a trial. Decisions belong with a neurologist and a genetic counselor. The clinical description follows GeneReviews (last updated 4 June 2020) and the other sources at the bottom. The research pipeline moves faster than that chapter; see Where things stand, reviewed 5 October 2026.

What it is

Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3, or SCA3, is also called Machado-Joseph disease. It is a progressive brain disease. The problem is a stretch of DNA in one gene, ATXN3, which makes a protein called ataxin-3. In that gene, the letters C-A-G are repeated. Everyone has some repeats. In SCA3 the repeat is expanded, and the protein that results harms nerve cells, especially in the cerebellum and connected pathways.

Laboratories do not all use the identical cutoffs, so a report has to be read with a counselor. GeneReviews summarizes the ranges roughly like this: normal alleles are about 12 to 44 repeats; intermediate alleles begin around 45 and can be unstable when passed on; full-penetrance disease alleles are often cited from about 60 to 87. The lower edge of “full penetrance” is not perfectly sharp.

How it is passed on

SCA3 is autosomal dominant. One expanded copy is enough. If a parent carries a pathogenic expansion, each child has a 50% chance of inheriting it, and a 50% chance of not inheriting it. That chance is the same for sons and daughters, and it is reset at each pregnancy. When the expansion is passed on, the disease does not skip that generation, though symptoms may not start until later in adulthood.

The repeat is unstable. It more often grows than shrinks from parent to child, which can mean earlier onset in the next generation. That pattern is called anticipation. Expansions are somewhat more likely when the expanded allele comes from the father, but the paternal effect is milder than in Huntington disease, and it is not a rule for any one child. Even when the repeat length is known, the age symptoms will start cannot be predicted precisely.

A short history

The disease was first written up in the 1970s in families of Azorean Portuguese ancestry. In 1972 Nakano, Dawson, and Spence described the Machado family, Portuguese emigrants to Massachusetts. In 1976 Rosenberg and colleagues described the Joseph family. Other Azorean families were reported in the same years. The names Machado and Joseph stayed attached to the disease. Coutinho and Andrade, studying families in the Azores, argued that these were one disorder with a wide range of signs, not several diseases.

In the early 1990s the gene was mapped to chromosome 14. In 1994 Kawaguchi and colleagues showed that the cause is an expanded CAG repeat in a gene then called MJD1, now ATXN3. A condition that had been named SCA3 turned out to be the same disease.

GeneReviews, citing a 2019 review, describes SCA3 as thought to be the most common spinocerebellar ataxia worldwide, on the order of 20% to 50% of SCA families in the series that review covers. It is the most common dominant ataxia in many places, including Portugal, Brazil, and China, and there are longstanding clusters among families from the Azores. It is uncommon in some other countries, including Italy and South Africa in the figures GeneReviews quotes. “Most common of a rare group” still means rare in the general population. There is no precise head count for the world.

What it does

The core feature is progressive ataxia: balance and coordination get worse, usually starting with walking. Speech often becomes slurred (dysarthria). Swallowing can become difficult (dysphagia), which matters because food or liquid can go into the airway. Eye movements are commonly affected: nystagmus, slow saccades, trouble moving the eyes together, and double vision.

Other features vary. Some people develop stiffness and brisk reflexes (spasticity), or dystonia, which is more often part of earlier-onset disease. Some develop neuropathy, muscle thinning, or parkinsonism. Sleep can be disrupted, including REM sleep behavior and restless legs. Pain and cramps occur. The mix changes as the years go on. GeneReviews notes that trying to force every person into one fixed subtype is not very useful, because the picture overlaps and can shift.

Thinking is not the center of the disease in the way it is in some other neurodegenerative conditions. Major cognitive decline is infrequent, and GeneReviews says people with SCA3 do not develop dementia in the usual course. Changes in planning, attention, visual processing, and mood, including depression, do occur. Those are real, and they are not “just the ataxia.”

When it starts

Age at onset varies widely, from childhood to late life. It most commonly falls in the second through fifth decades of life. In a large group of affected people from the Azores, the mean onset was 37. A large Brazilian study found a mean onset of about 36. So the 30s and 40s are a frequent range, not a boundary. Longer CAG repeats tend to go with earlier onset. Other genetic and non-genetic factors also play a part, which is why two people with similar repeat lengths can differ.

How it progresses

The course is gradual, over years. GeneReviews says walking often becomes hard enough that people need a cane, walker, or wheelchair about 10 to 15 years after onset, with a great deal of individual difference. Later on, speech and swallowing difficulty are usually severe, and a weak cough is common.

Life expectancy is not one number. GeneReviews reports deaths from pulmonary complications and cachexia (severe wasting) anywhere from about 6 to 29 years after onset. In the Brazilian survival study, mean survival after onset was about 21 years. That average is not a forecast for a particular person. Earlier onset and longer repeats were linked, in that study, with shorter survival. Swallowing trouble is one reason pulmonary complications, including aspiration, are watched so closely. It is not the only late risk.

How it is diagnosed

The diagnosis rests on the neurologic picture plus a laboratory test that counts the CAG repeats in ATXN3. A standard exome, genome, or many multigene panels that only sequence the gene can miss a repeat expansion. The test has to be one designed to measure that repeat. Other ataxias, and some acquired causes of ataxia, can look similar until the gene test is done. Absence of a known family history does not rule SCA3 out.

Care today

As of the GeneReviews chapter, and still as of the October 2026 review on the pipeline page, no drug is approved to slow or stop the underlying disease. Care is supportive, and it is worth doing. A movement-disorders or ataxia neurologist usually coordinates a team: physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech and swallow therapy, eye care, nutrition, mood, and primary care. Exercise and fall prevention help function. They are not a proven way to delay the molecular disease in someone who carries the expansion and has no symptoms. Medicines may help pieces of the picture, such as spasticity, dystonia, cramps, sleep, or depression. Those choices are individual.

For family planning, testing an embryo or a pregnancy can be discussed with a genetics team. That is covered, with the same caution, on Where things stand. It does not change the genetic status of a child who is already born.

Where to get help

  • National Ataxia Foundation, SCA3 — patient information, community, and research programs. The SCA3 pipeline page tracks treatment programs, and CRC-SCA is a natural-history network in the United States and Canada.
  • GeneReviews: Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 3 — the standard clinical reference for inheritance, repeat ranges, and care. Confirm the revision date on the page itself.
  • A genetic counselor, especially before any predictive test or a pregnancy.
  • A neurologist at an ataxia or movement-disorders clinic, if symptoms are present or a family needs a medical home.
